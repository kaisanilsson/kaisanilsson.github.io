Introduction

The data I will analyse is “Household Tales of Brothers Grimm” by Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm, translated by Margaret Hunt. It includes 210 stories, from the very classic (“Hansel and Gretel” or “Rapunzel”) to the more obscure (“The Shroud”). The Brothers Grimm collected old German folk and fairy tales over a period of about four decades. As they collected new stories and published new collections, they revised, dropped, and added tales at will, to support their goals of preserving German culture reflecting current contexts1.

There are many questions that can be asked and answered about these stories through text analysis, but the ones I’m most interested in lean into the role of the Grimm’s Tales in preserving and defining fairy tales. Is there a format to beginnings and endings of stories that seems to connected to the well known, formulaic “Once upon a time” and “Happily ever after”? What about other word formulas, like the wicked stepmother? The Grimm’s fairy tales are often claimed to be dark–what does sentiment analysis say about this? How distinctive, really, are the stories?

One of the most classic fairy tale beginnings is “Once upon a time…”. Since this collection was translated into English, there are some differences in names and phrases that may not have been in the original text. Still, there are some formulas for starting each tale.

The most common beginning phrase by far is “There was once” followed by “There were once”, which is somewhat surprising given the stereotypical fairy tale beginning. However, that quintessential phrase returns in the third position, and there is clearly still a formula to the beginnings.

Code 
beginnings <- grimm_raw |>
  slice(218:22228) |> 
  mutate(chap_title = str_detect(text, "\\d"), 
         sentence_lead = lead(text), 
         first_words = ifelse(chap_title == TRUE, 
                              str_extract(sentence_lead, "^([^ ]+ ){3}"),
                              NA)) |>
  group_by(first_words) |>
  drop_na() |>
  summarize(count = n()) |>
  arrange(desc(count)) |>
  filter(count > 2)

This code shows two other regular expressions I used: “\\d” and “^([^ ]+ ){3}”.

Code 
endings <- grimm_raw |>
  slice(218:22228) |> 
  mutate(chap_title = str_detect(text, "\\d"), 
         sentence_lag = lag(text, default = "none"), 
         last_words = ifelse(chap_title == TRUE, 
                              str_extract(sentence_lag, "([^ ]+ [^ ]+)$"),
                              NA)) |>
  group_by(last_words) |>
  drop_na() |>
  summarize(count = n()) |>
  filter(count > 1) |>
  arrange(desc(count))

This code displays the regular expression “([^ ]+ [^ ]+)$”.

Word Association

We can extend our study of names to include many more words with a words association network graph. Excluding basic, common words like “and” (and those specific to this text, like “thou”), we can see the relationships between words that appear next to each other often. “king’s son” and “king’s daughter” have the darkest arrows, which follows from the initial discussion of roles as names, and the number of stories that must have those as main characters. The networks with only two nodes also reveal typical characters or concepts in stories, like “wise woman” and “spinning wheel”.

Sentiment Analysis

In comparison to some other fairy tale collections, and often the popular perception of tales from children’s books or Disney, the Grimm’s tales have a reputation for being violent and sometimes vengeful; one of the most classic examples is in “Snow White”, when the wicked stepmother was forced to dance until she died in shoes of red-hot iron. I’m interested in asking what negative topics were focused on and what language was used–is it death, or are there other punishment words? Among positive words, I wanted to see if there was any clear juxtaposition with the most common negative words.

In the figure below, it’s clear that the most common words with negative sentiments are related to death, or hell–the devil. “fell” is an interesting case, since it is used in a negative way, but also to describe fortune, or the fall of hair, which I would argue are not negative. The positive-sentiment words are interesting, since there don’t appear to be many contradictions, other than rich and poor. Instead, the top few are more judgment terms used to describe characters, rather than actions or concepts like the negative sentiment words.

Overall, some of my initial assumptions about this analysis were proved true–there is a sort of formula to beginnings of the tales, although “Once upon a time” is not as prevalent. Through the plots of word sentiments and word association, some of the overall subjects become more clear–some of the stories may focus on death or the devil, but there are many more mentions of good or positive ideas as well. Both plots show how many things are described as “good” or “golden”.

With all of this analysis, looking beyond classic ideas of fairytales about princesses or fair maidens, it’s clear to see why the tales of the Brothers Grimm have stayed so long in our culture. The positive sentiments far outweigh the negative, and there is a simplicity to stories about animals or sons or daughters or brothers that could be about anyone, even the sons or daughters or brothers of kings. The stories lasted long before being written down and still have until now for a reason: maybe, in the first words of the collection, we all want to read about “the old times when wishing still helped one”2.
